Get to Know Add Shader: How It Differs from Mix Shader

Add Shader looks similar to Mix Shader — both combine two shaders — but Add has no Fac ratio: the two inputs are summed directly, not blended. This tutorial demonstrates the difference directly with two Emission nodes: for the same two inputs, Add's result is noticeably brighter than Mix at a 50/50 split.

1. Step 1: Two Emission Nodes, Combined with Add Shader

Add two Emission nodes: set one to red, one to green, both with Strength 1. Add an Add Shader (Shader category), connect both Emission nodes into its two inputs, then connect it to Material Output's Surface.

Since Emission has no Base Color, Add Shader here simply sums red and green light directly — you should see yellow (red + green).

2. Step 2: Raise One Strength, Watch the Balance Shift

Raise the red Emission node's Strength above 2. The color noticeably shifts toward orange-red.

Because Add Shader genuinely sums the two lights together, whichever side has higher strength pulls the final color toward it, unlike Mix Shader's fixed 50/50 split.

3. Step 3: Wire Up a Mix Shader Version to Compare

Add a Mix Shader, and connect the same red and green Emission nodes into its two inputs (leave Fac at the default 0.5).

⚠️ Don't connect it to Material Output yet — this step just wires up the Mix version so you can compare it in the next step.

4. Step 4: Switch to Mix Shader and Compare Brightness

Reconnect Material Output's Surface to the Mix Shader's output instead. The screen noticeably dims.

At Fac=0.5, Mix Shader gives you 'half of each' (the average), while Add Shader gives you the full sum, so for the same inputs, Add is twice as bright as Mix.

This is why Add Shader is typically used to layer extra reflection (like Sheen) on top of a material, rather than blending two complete materials together.
